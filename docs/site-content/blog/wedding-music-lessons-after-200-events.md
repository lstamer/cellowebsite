## Body

### Fewer Mistakes, More Intention: How to Think About Your Wedding Music

Most couples don’t need more musical ideas. They need fewer avoidable mistakes.

After more than 200 wedding performances, I see the same issues repeat. They’re rarely disasters. They’re small decisions made too late, or made without understanding what the music is actually meant to do in the room.

Live music is not there to impress people in the abstract. It is there to shape a moment. When it works, guests don’t think, *“That was a nice extra.”* They simply feel that the ceremony had atmosphere, that the room had elegance, and that the day felt intentional.

## 1. The biggest mistake: choosing music before choosing the feeling

Most couples start with songs. That’s understandable, but it can create strange results.

A song may be meaningful to you in the car, on headphones, or at the end of a night out. That doesn’t automatically mean it will serve the ceremony well.

A better first question is:

> **What should this part of the day feel like?**

For example:

- Do you want the entrance to feel **still and reverent**, **warm and intimate**, or **light and joyful**?
- Do you want pre-ceremony music that **settles people quietly**, or music that **signals that something special is about to happen**?

Once you know the emotional job each section needs to do, choosing specific pieces becomes much easier and more coherent.

## 2. Timing matters more than people expect

A beautiful piece can still be the wrong choice if its length and shape don’t match what’s physically happening in the ceremony.

Common problems:

- Choosing a processional without considering **walking speed**, **aisle length**, or **multiple entrances** (e.g. wedding party, then partner, then parents).
- Assuming the signing needs a full “concert piece” when it usually needs **flexible music** that can be naturally extended or brought to a close at the right moment.

This is where experience matters. Someone needs to understand how to **adapt in real time**—to loop, shorten, or reshape the music—without it ever feeling abrupt or uncertain to your guests.

## 3. Volume should support the room, not fight it

Not every venue wants the same sound. A cellar, vineyard lawn, church, hotel courtyard, and private garden all respond very differently to live instruments.

Couples often imagine live music as automatically “soft and elegant,” but in reality:

- **Placement** of the musician
- **Guest count** and how spread out people are
- **Background noise** (traffic, fountains, catering, wind)
- Whether **amplification** is used

all change how the music actually lands.

The goal is not “make the cello louder at all costs.” The goal is to make it **present, clear, and appropriate** to the moment.

That usually means deciding early whether the event will be:

- purely **acoustic**
- **lightly amplified** for clarity
- or **dependent on a PA** for coverage across a large or noisy space

## 4. Personal songs can work beautifully, but they need translation

A meaningful contemporary song can be a wonderful choice. The key is arranging it so it still feels natural on the cello and suits the moment where it appears.

Some songs:

- Have a **strong, singable melody** that carries well instrumentally.
- Others rely mostly on **lyrics**, **production**, or **rhythm**. Those can still work, but they need careful handling.

The question isn’t only, *“Do we love this song?”* It’s also:

> **Will this still speak when played instrumentally, in this room, at this point in the day?**

A good arranger or performer will tell you honestly when a song will translate well and when it might feel thin or awkward without vocals.

## 5. The best music plans are simple, not crowded

Another pattern I see is overbuilding the musical plan.

Couples feel pressure to “optimise” every minute, so they create:

- too many pieces
- too many transitions
- too many “special moments” that all compete for emotional importance

In practice, the strongest ceremonies usually rely on a **clean, simple shape** with a few clear anchors:

- **Pre-ceremony:** calm, welcoming music as guests arrive
- **Processional:** one clear, confident entrance choice
- **Signing / register:** flexible music that can expand or resolve naturally
- **Recessional:** something that releases the room with energy and confidence

You don’t need a complicated soundtrack. You need the **right structure**.

## 6. The real value of a professional is not just the playing

People sometimes think they are booking “songs.” In reality, they are booking **judgment**.

A professional should be able to:

- Suggest suitable options quickly once they understand your taste and venue
- Recognise when a song **won’t translate well** and offer alternatives
- Adapt to **delays, last-minute changes, longer aisle walks**, or a coordinator who suddenly changes the order
- Stay calm and make the whole thing feel **effortless** from your perspective

That calm, flexible presence is often what couples remember most—not because it’s flashy, but because it quietly removes stress from a part of the day that already carries a lot of emotion.

## 7. What I would tell every couple

If you’re planning your wedding music, keep these priorities in order:

1. **Choose the feeling before the song.** Decide what each moment should feel like.
1. **Match the music to the timing.** Think about walking, signing, and transitions.
1. **Think about the room, not just the playlist.** Consider acoustics, volume, and amplification.
1. **Keep the structure clean and uncluttered.** A few strong choices beat a crowded plan.
1. **Work with someone who can guide, not just perform.** You’re booking judgment, not just a set list.

When music is treated as part of the atmosphere rather than an afterthought, the whole day feels more considered. That is usually the difference between music that is merely pleasant and music people still talk about years later.
